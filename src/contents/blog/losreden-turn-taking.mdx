> *Disclosure: I'm a Solutions Architect at DigitalOcean, so when I mention DigitalOcean products it's an informed-insider view, not a neutral one — I flag those moments clearly and keep the analysis vendor-neutral wherever the topic allows. The opinions here are my own.*

Language learners pause. They pause in the middle of a sentence while they look for a word, and they pause
just as often right after a sentence that is grammatically complete: "Ich komme aus Taiwan. [2 s] Also, ich
wohne jetzt in Köln." To a turn detector trained on fluent speech, that second pause sounds like the end of
a turn.

My mock speaking exam, **Losreden** ([part 1](/blog/losreden-livekit-voice-exam) describes how it is built on LiveKit Agents),
has an AI exam partner. In an exam, the most unfair thing she can do is take the floor in the middle of the
candidate's thinking pause. So I set up a lab to find out how often she did that, and which settings stop it.

The lab took 58 calls and cost $8.09. It also found two bugs in my own harness. The result I did not expect:
**the LLM's speed is part of the turn-taking policy**. With the old settings, the partner cut into learners'
pauses twice as often after midnight as before it, because the LLM provider was faster at night.

Everything here is from livekit-agents 1.8.11, a scripted learner and telc B1 Teil 1 (getting to know each
other). The full build log, with every run, is `docs/turnlab-buildlog.md` in the repository.

### TL;DR

- **The test:** eight LiveKit turn-taking settings, 40 calls with a scripted, synthesized B1 learner who pauses the same way every time, measured by a hidden observer in the room. The whole lab took 58 calls and $8.09.
- **A 3.0 s `false_interruption_timeout` was the clearest win:** the partner resumed over a learner's "Äh, ähm." in 15 of 15 cases at 2.0 s and 3 of 15 at 3.0 s.
- **Adaptive barge-in was worse for learners,** and dynamic endpointing did not help within one part of an exam: it starts low, learns slowly, and resets at every `update_agent()`.
- **The LLM's speed is part of the policy.** With `min_delay` below the LLM's time to first token, the old settings cut into 29 % of finished-sentence pauses before midnight and 64 % after, because the provider got faster.
- **What I chose:** fixed 2.0 / 5.0 s, timeout 3.0 s, VAD barge-in. It cut into 13 % of all pauses against 50 %, and the price is a reply about 1 s later.

### The advice I started from

A thread on the LiveKit community forum about turn detection for non-native speakers gave four suggestions:

1. Use **dynamic endpointing** (`mode: dynamic, min_delay: 0.5, max_delay: 5.0`), so the agent learns how
   long this speaker pauses.
2. Raise **`false_interruption_timeout`** (default 2.0 s) to about the length of a learner's pauses.
3. Prefer **VAD barge-in over the adaptive interruption model**, which was presumably trained on native
   speakers who backchannel. Learners hardly do.
4. **Measure against where the speaker really stopped**, not against the framework's own end-of-speech.

All four sound right, and I could have flipped them on and called it done. I read the installed source
first, because it costs nothing.

### What the source said that the thread did not

Five things changed the experiment before I made a single call:

- **The adaptive interruption model was never on.** When the interruption mode is not set and the worker
  runs in production mode, `AgentActivity._resolve_interruption_detection` returns no detector. Every exam
  job logs `adaptive interruption is disabled by default in production mode`. So my exams already used VAD
  barge-in. For me, the condition to test was *turning adaptive on*.
- **Dynamic endpointing forgets at every handoff.** `AgentActivity.start` builds a new endpointing object,
  and `session.update_agent()` starts a new activity. My exam switches agents at every part (three in telc
  B1, seven steps in Goethe B1), so what it learned about a candidate in Teil 1 is gone in Teil 2. I added
  a carry-over: read the learned value before the switch and pass it on with
  `session.update_options(endpointing_opts={"min_delay": learned})`.
- **Dynamic starts lower than what I had.** It learns `min_delay` upward from the configured value (an
  exponential moving average with alpha 0.9, so each pause moves it 10 % of the way) and never goes below it.
  My fixed delay was 1.0 s, and the suggested floor is 0.5 s. Until it has seen a few pauses, it is *quicker*
  to cut in.
- **Which pauses `min_delay` is for.** With the turn detector, `max_delay` (5.0 s) applies when the model
  thinks the sentence is unfinished, and `min_delay` when it thinks the turn may be over. So pauses in the
  middle of a sentence are mostly the detector's job. `min_delay` matters after a *finished* sentence, which
  is exactly where a learner stops to think.
- **What counts as a false interruption.** On a barge-in the agent pauses its audio. A final transcript with
  words turns that into a real interruption. Only a barge-in without words ends in
  `false_interruption_timeout`: a lone "Ähm" that the STT drops. Then the agent resumes its sentence, over a
  learner who is still looking for the next word. One more detail: `interruption.min_duration` is 0.5 s, so a
  short "Ähm" does not interrupt at all. My first synthesized "Ähm." was 0.30 s long. The script uses longer
  fillers.

### The harness: a learner who pauses the same way every time

An LLM bot would pause differently in every run, so I wrote a **scripted learner** instead. It has 16 turns
for telc B1 Teil 1. Each chunk of speech is synthesized once with Deepgram Aura-2 and cached, with the
leading and trailing silence cut. Every condition therefore hears the same audio with the same silences:

- 9 pauses after a finished sentence, 7 in the middle of one, and 3 after a filler, each 1.2–3.0 s;
- 4 turns that start while the partner is talking (a barge-in) and then pause.

The learner is a plain room participant. It sends a steady 10 ms frame stream, like a microphone. It knows
when she has finished from her `lk.agent.state`, and it reacts after 0.7 s.

**Measured from outside.** The numbers come from a hidden observer in the room. It runs an energy VAD on both
tracks and stamps each frame on arrival. On LiveKit Cloud, a smoothed frame clock was 90 ms off where
arrival stamps were 3–5 ms off; I found that when I validated the observer on Cloud. A pause counts as **cut** if her voice
is heard after the pause began and before the learner's next chunk is over. For a barge-in I measured how
long she kept talking, and whether she started again inside the same turn (*resumed over*). I checked that
against the framework's own `agent_false_interruption(resumed=true)` events.

Every new setting went into config with the old behaviour as the default. A test checks that the defaults
still produce exactly the old options.

### Two bugs in my own harness

The first smoke run (old settings, $0.16) gave a loud number: the partner took the turn in **8 of 9** pauses
after a finished sentence. That would have made a great headline. It was my bug.

Printing the timeline, instead of trusting the table, showed the learner's clock was bimodal: its lag to the
observer was either 0.17 s or about 0.8 s. The network was not the cause; the end-of-chunk lag was a steady
0.14 ± 0.01 s. Aura-2 starts some clips with a 10 ms click at −42 dBFS and then about 0.75 s of silence, and
my trim kept everything after the first voiced window. So some "exact" pauses were 0.75 s longer than
scripted. With the trim starting at the first 50 ms run of speech, the baseline cut in after **1 of 9**
finished sentences, not 8.

The second bug: barge-ins came too late. The learner judged "she is talking" from her audio energy, which
drops between her sentences, so 2 of 4 barge-ins landed in a gap or after she had finished. The learner now
uses her `lk.agent.state`, which says "speaking" for the whole reply.

The other lesson from the smoke runs: one run per condition says nothing. Two runs of the *same* settings
cut into 21 % and 32 % of all pauses. From then on, every condition got 5 runs, interleaved (A1 B1 C1 … A2
B2 …), about 5 minutes and $0.14 each.

### Eight conditions, 40 calls

Per condition: 45 pauses after a finished sentence, 35 in the middle of one, 15 after a filler and 20
barge-ins. *Gap* is the time from the learner's last word to her voice.

| | Setting | Cut-in after a finished sentence | Mid-sentence | Resumed over the learner (barge-ins) | Gap median / p90 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | old: fixed 1.0 / 5.0 s, VAD barge-in, timeout 2.0 s | 29 % | 0 % | 75 % | 2.95 / 6.75 s |
| B | dynamic 0.5 / 5.0 s | 33 % | 3 % | 75 % | 2.81 / 6.72 s |
| C | B + timeout 3.0 s | 36 % | 9 % | **15 %** | 2.85 / 7.01 s |
| D | C + adaptive barge-in | 29 % | 14 % | 90 % | 2.80 / 5.93 s |
| E | fixed 2.0 / 5.0 s | 22 % | 9 % | 70 % | 3.95 / 5.85 s |
| F | A, resume off | **80 %** | 20 % | 0 % | 2.19 / 5.82 s |
| G | A + timeout 3.0 s | 64 % (see below) | 9 % | 25 % | 2.63 / 5.92 s |
| H | fixed 2.0 / 5.0 s + timeout 3.0 s | 22 % | 3 % | 30 % | 3.67 / 6.65 s |

Point by point against the forum advice:

1. **Dynamic endpointing did not help within one part of an exam.** B against A, 33 % against 29 %, is inside
   the run-to-run spread. It starts at 0.5 s, passes the old fixed 1.0 s only after about two minutes
   (1.9–3.2 min), and ends the four-minute script at 1.2–1.5 s, nowhere near 2.0 s. telc B1's Teil 1 lasts
   about 3 minutes. The carry-over fixes the reset at each part, not the slowness.

   ![The learned min_delay over 15 dynamic runs](/images/blog/losreden/learning.png)
2. **A 3.0 s `false_interruption_timeout` is the clearest win.** A learner who says "Äh, ähm." and then
   searches for words for 2.4–3.0 s had the partner start her sentence again over them in 15 of 15 cases at
   2.0 s, and in 3 of 15 at 3.0 s (B against C). The thread was right.
3. **Adaptive barge-in is worse for learners, as the thread guessed** (D against C). It classifies "Äh, ähm."
   and "Ja, aber …" as backchannels, so she keeps talking for 0.9 s (0.6 s with VAD) and resumes in 18 of 20
   barge-ins. None of those went through the false-interruption timer: a backchannel verdict resumes her at
   once, so a longer timeout does not help there.
4. **Measuring from the room changed the answer.** Condition F turned off `resume_false_interruption`, and
   cut-ins after finished sentences went from 29 % to 80 %. She is not quicker in F. With resume on, a reply
   committed just before the learner goes on is *paused before its first audio frame* and then dropped when
   the learner's words arrive, so the learner never hears it. With resume off nothing pauses, and she gets
   0.1–0.6 s of speech out before the learner's voice interrupts her. The framework's own `interrupted` count
   looks the same either way. Only a listener in the room hears the difference.

   ![Resume on or off](/images/blog/losreden/resume.png)

One trace shows why even a 2.0 s `min_delay` can cut into a 2.4 s pause. The learner stops at 0.00. The
framework's user state turns `listening` at 0.07. The turn is committed at 2.07, the agent is `speaking` at
2.15, and her voice is heard at 2.52, 0.12 s after the learner went on. Preemptive generation had the reply
ready at the commit, so the audible cut-in comes at about `min_delay` + 0.45 s.

![One cut-in, step by step](/images/blog/losreden/cutin-trace.png)

### The finding I did not expect: the LLM's speed is part of the policy

Condition G cut in after 64 % of finished sentences, against 29 % for A. G only adds the longer timeout,
which has nothing to do with endpointing; C against B showed no such jump. The difference was the hour. A to
E ran from 21:57 to midnight, F to H from 00:02 to 01:15. After midnight, the LLM on DigitalOcean Serverless
Inference got faster: its median time to first token went from 1.05 s to 0.87 s.

So I ran a control in one window (01:16–02:36): A, G, H and a new I (fixed 1.5 s), interleaved, 4 runs each.

| | Setting | Cut-in after a finished sentence | All pauses | Resumed over "Ähm" | Her voice per cut-in | Gap median / p90 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | old: fixed 1.0 s, timeout 2.0 s | **64 %** (29 % before midnight) | 50 % | 100 % | 0.55 s | 2.64 / 6.60 s |
| G | fixed 1.0 s, timeout 3.0 s | 61 % | 36 % | 25 % | 0.53 s | 2.72 / 6.57 s |
| I | fixed 1.5 s, timeout 3.0 s | 44 % | 28 % | 25 % | 0.55 s | 3.10 / 5.82 s |
| **H** | **fixed 2.0 s, timeout 3.0 s** | **22 %** | **13 %** | **17 %** | **0.22 s** | 3.68 / 6.37 s |

![Cut-ins by min_delay and hour](/images/blog/losreden/turn-hour.png)

The mechanism: with `min_delay` at 1.0 s, the reply is *not* ready when the turn is committed, because it
needs about 1.05 s. She starts about 1.2 s after the commit, so the learner hears her about 2.7 s after they
stopped. The old settings' apparent patience came from the LLM being slow. When the provider is faster, she
starts about 0.35 s after the commit and lands inside the learner's pause. With `min_delay` at 2.0 s, the
preemptively generated reply is always ready at the commit, so she is heard about 2.7 s after the learner
stopped whatever the provider does that hour. The fixed 2.0 s delay gave 22 % before midnight (E) and 22 %
after it (H, in both windows).

**The lesson for anyone tuning this:** if `min_delay` is below your LLM's time to first token, your real
delay is the LLM's, and it moves with the provider's load. Measure at more than one time of day.

### What I chose, and what it costs

```yaml
telc:
  turn_taking: {mode: fixed, min_delay: 2.0, max_delay: 5.0}
  interruption: {mode: null, false_interruption_timeout: 3.0}
```

- **Fixed, not dynamic.** Dynamic starts below what learners need and learns too slowly for a 3–6 minute
  part. Dynamic mode and the carry-over stay in the code as options.
- **VAD barge-in, not adaptive.** A learner's filler is not a backchannel.
- **Resume stays on.** It is what keeps a reply that comes just too early from being heard at all.
- **The price:** she answers about 1 s later, a median of 3.7 s from the learner's last word to her voice
  (p90 6.4 s). For an exam partner that is the right side to err on. telc's own guidance is that the examiner
  lets the candidate finish. If it feels slow in practice, 1.5 s (condition I) is the next step down, at
  about twice the cut-ins.

In the same hour, the chosen settings cut into 13 % of all pauses against 50 % for the old ones. After a
learner's "Ähm", she resumed over them 17 % of the time against 100 %. The settings went live on
2026-11-11 after one whole-exam check.

**What this does not cover.** One voice, one script and one task type, with the clean audio of a synthesized
learner. Real learners pause in more places and for longer. Teachers rating real transcripts is the next
check (see part 1).

### What I would ask LiveKit for

1. **Keep what dynamic endpointing learned across `update_agent()`**, or document that it resets. A
   multi-agent flow loses it at every handoff today.
2. **Say in the docs that `min_delay` is a floor, not the delay.** Below the LLM's time to first token, the
   provider's latency sets the real delay, and that changes with the hour.
3. **An "audible" flag on interruption metrics.** A reply that was paused before its first frame and dropped
   counts as `interrupted` just like one the user heard. A flag would let people see what F showed without
   building an observer.
4. **A note on adaptive interruption and non-native speech.** Fillers like "Äh, ähm." are classified as
   backchannels. That is fine for fluent speakers and wrong for learners. It is off by default in production
   mode, which is the right default, but the reason is worth a sentence.

Reading the source before the first call saved me from testing a setting that was already off, and printing
timelines instead of trusting the summary table caught both of my own bugs. The result I was not looking for
only showed up when I ran the same test at a different hour.
